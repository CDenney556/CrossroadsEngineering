S Kipling St & W Stanford Ave, Lakewood, Colorado. August 31, 2026, 10:11 PM.

Over two minutes at a red light, with nobody coming.

During our field observation, a Jeep sat at the side-street red for more than two minutes. Kipling was empty for 62 seconds of that wait, including one gap of over 20 seconds. The driver gave up, turned right, and made a U-turn down the road.

We're Crossroad Engineering. This is our design for making the signals cities already own adapt to the traffic that's actually there.

The problem

  • 5–10% of all U.S. traffic delay happens at traffic signals [1]
  • 60.2% of trips start off-peak, outside the hours signal timing is tuned for [2]
  • 56 gaps of 12 seconds or more on Kipling in one hour, while side-street drivers waited 48–120 seconds

Twelve seconds is about what it takes to serve one side-street car: the yellows, the all-red, and the six-second minimum green. Kipling had 56 of those gaps in the hour we watched, and the side street still waited.

The signal at Kipling and Stanford isn't a dumb timer. It detects cars. But it runs a time-of-day plan built around a fixed cycle, so a car that arrives at the wrong point in the cycle waits for it to come around. Lakewood switches it to free operation only between 10:30 PM and 5:00 AM.

The fix: adaptive signal control

Adaptive signals time themselves to the traffic actually arriving, instead of following a fixed plan. The idea isn't new. Almost no one uses it.

  • <1% of U.S. signals have adaptive control [3]
  • $70,594 for our system over 10 years at one intersection. Other adaptive systems: $39,548–$120,469 [5]
  • 350 side-street cars a day to break even

What holds cities back is cost, the expertise to run it, and no easy way to measure the payoff [3]. So our goal was narrower than a full adaptive system:

How might we reduce delays for drivers at signalized intersections during off-peak hours?

Our answer is an add-on that works with the controller a city already has. It only steps in during low-demand hours, when fixed timing does the most damage, and it never touches safety timing or emergency preemption.

How it works

  1. Pucks in the road, in pairs every 100 m out to 500 m, time each car on the way to the light, so the system knows where the gaps will be before they arrive.
  2. The sensor head on the mast arm measures each car's range and speed over the last 150 m, so the prediction is sharpest right before the light.
  3. The cabinet computer predicts when each car will reach the stop bar and finds gaps long enough to serve the side street.
  4. The existing controller gets a standard call and changes the light. Its minimum greens, clearances, and preemption all stay in charge.

The three parts

  • Puck

    A 6-inch sealed cylinder flush with the road. A magnetometer senses each car bending Earth's magnetic field, and a mesh radio reports it. Four D cells last 10+ years.

    Cameron Denney, Isaac Lucero

  • Sensor head

    Two cameras 1 m apart on a carbon-fiber bar, hung from the existing mast arm. It measures each car's range and speed from 25 to 150 m out.

    Simon Wright

  • Cabinet computer

    An industrial computer that fits in the spare space of a standard signal cabinet and talks to the existing controller over NTCIP 1202.

    Kyle Lindenmeyer

Where it stands

The detection hardware and the arrival math work. In simulation, every car arrived inside its predicted window, and in our parking-lot test the puck counted 20 of 20 passes exactly once.

The switching rules need more work. On the hour we observed at Kipling and Stanford, where traffic comes in waves, they cut the average side-street wait by 23%. On a full weekday of steady, busy traffic, today's timing beat them in every hour. We're retuning them with hourly data from the City of Lakewood.

What stakeholders told us

Kelly Dunne, PE, PTOE, City of Lakewood: a fix has to be reasonably priced, work in any weather, and leave peak-hour timing alone. So the system runs off-peak only, and the pucks work in snow and ice.

Jim Quinn, retired Denver firefighter: preemption already clears intersections with a "quick yellow, quick red." So preemption always overrides our system.

Paul Kuenne, delivery driver: the failure he runs into most is sitting at a red with few or no cars coming. That's exactly the case we serve.

Cameron Denney, Kyle Lindenmeyer, Simon Wright, Isaac Lucero
