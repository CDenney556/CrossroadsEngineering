Design

The system has three parts, in the order the data moves: the puck in the road, the sensor head on the mast arm, and the computer in the signal cabinet. Each one was designed by a different team member, and each section below covers what it does, how it works, and what it's made of. The full report has every calculation and decision matrix.

In the sketches, teal is our hardware and gray is equipment that's already at the intersection.

Puck

Cameron Denney

Pucks go in the travel lane every 100 m, out to 300–500 m from the stop bar. Each one counts the cars that pass over it and reports them to the intersection, so the computer knows about each car, and each gap, well before it arrives.

How it detects a car

A car is a large mass of steel, and it bends Earth's magnetic field as it passes. A 3-axis magnetometer 50 mm under the road measures that change. The puck checks the field 10 times a second while it waits. When the change crosses a threshold, it switches to 200 readings a second until the car has gone, timestamps the detection, and sends a short packet to the intersection over a 915 MHz LoRa mesh radio.

The intersection sends out a regular time beacon that keeps every puck's clock in sync. That lets the computer work out speed from the difference between two pucks' timestamps, not from when the radio packets happened to arrive.

Why the layers are in this order

  • Antenna on top, under a 2.5 mm plastic lid, for the clearest path to the radio network.
  • Magnetometer 50 mm down, on nylon standoffs, kept as far as possible from the batteries and the boards that carry current. Hardware near it is nylon or brass.
  • Batteries at the bottom. Their low-magnetic cells add only about 0.25 µT at the sensor, a fixed offset the empty-road baseline removes.

Puck electronics

The electronics are split across separate boards, so each one can be designed and tested on its own, and so the radio and its return currents stay away from the magnetometer. The compute, radio, and BMS boards share the same 60 × 60 mm outline and one set of threaded rods.

  • Compute board: the microcontroller that runs detection, plus the 3.0 V regulator and the 32.768 kHz clock crystal.
  • Magnetometer board: the sensor on its own small board, on a short cable so it can sit as far from the battery and radio as possible.
  • Radio board: the LoRa module and a Nordic nRF52840 that runs the mesh networking.
  • BMS board: part of the power subsystem, sitting right on the cells.

Compute board schematic

The schematic is complete and passes KiCad's electrical rules check with no errors. Three connectors tie the boards together:

  • J1 to the power subsystem: battery supply, ground, the fuel gauge's I²C bus, and status lines.
  • J2 to the magnetometer board: SPI and the sensor's interrupt.
  • J3 to the radio board: a UART link plus wake, interrupt, beacon sync, and reset lines.

Still to update: the schematic was drawn before the battery management board was revised, so J1 still needs to be brought in line with the new BMS.

Puck power: battery pack and BMS

Cameron Denney, Isaac Lucero

The power subsystem has to run the puck for 10 years with no outside power and no service. It holds the cells, protects them from each other, tracks how much charge is left, and warns before the battery runs low.

  • Four Saft LS33600LM D cells (lithium-thionyl chloride, 68 Ah total) stand upright in parallel in an 82 mm holder.
  • Each cell has a fuse and two ideal diodes in series. Primary cells must never be charged, and in a parallel pack a weak cell could be charged by its neighbors. The second diode covers the case where the first one fails.
  • A fuel gauge wakes for a few seconds every 8 hours, timed to a radio burst so it has a current pulse to measure.
  • A separate supervisor chip flags the battery if it drops below 3.2 V, even if the firmware has stopped.
  • 20.2 yr until the pack hits its 20% reserve at the normal load (10-year target)
  • 10.4 yr if the puck relayed for its neighbors the entire time
  • 1.9 µA drawn by the BMS itself in standby

Sensor head

Simon Wright

The sensor head is the high-accuracy half of detection. It mounts on the existing mast arm, tracks each car as it approaches, and sends its range, speed, and predicted arrival time to the cabinet computer. It also gives the system a view of the crosswalks, so the computer never asks for a change while someone is still crossing.

How it works

Two cameras spaced apart see the road from slightly different angles, the way your two eyes do. Comparing the two images gives the distance to each car, and tracking that distance over time gives its speed. A LiDAR adds a second range measurement, mainly to keep the camera pair calibrated.

Main parts

  • Two D3 Embedded IMX390 cameras: 1920 × 1080 at 30 fps, HDR, sealed to IP67, rated −40 to +85 °C.
  • Benewake TF03 LiDAR for range.
  • TI TDA4VM processor with a built-in stereo engine, so the image processing happens on the pole and only small results go down to the cabinet.
  • One PoE++ cable carries both power and data from the cabinet.

Cabinet computer

Kyle Lindenmeyer

The cabinet computer makes the timing decisions. It takes in puck detections from a radio node over USB and the sensor head's results over Ethernet, predicts when each car will reach the stop bar, and sends the existing controller a standard call when the side street can go. It connects to the controller over NTCIP 1202, the protocol Lakewood's Econolite Cobalt controllers already speak.

Choosing the computer

A signal cabinet has to keep running from −34 to +74 °C, so temperature was weighted at 30% in the decision matrix. The Toradex Verdin iMX8M Plus on an Ivy Plus carrier board is rated −40 to +85 °C and scored 4.55 out of 5, ahead of the Raspberry Pi 5 (2.80, rated only 0 to +70 °C) and the NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano developer kit (2.35, not rated for a cabinet), even though it costs the most.

Software

The timing decisions run in C++ on FreeRTOS, on the Verdin's real-time core, so they happen on a predictable schedule. Linux on the main cores handles logging, alerts, networking, and updates.

Predicting arrivals

Pucks are installed in pairs. Each puck timestamps a car at its first magnetic peak, so both pucks in a pair time the same point on the car, whatever its length. From there the computer:

  1. gets the car's speed from the time between the two pucks in a pair,
  2. estimates when it will reach the stop bar at that speed,
  3. updates that estimate at every pair of pucks it crosses, along with its average speed and whether it's speeding up or slowing down,
  4. treats each arrival time as a window, since drivers drift a few mph either way,
  5. and measures gaps between cars from the safe edges of those windows.

When a gap is long enough to serve the side street, the computer places the call. If any sensor or the computer itself fails, the signal simply goes back to running on its own.
